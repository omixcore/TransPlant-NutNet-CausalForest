# Cover Letter — Global Change Biology

Dear Editors,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript "**Stable richness, shifting composition: causal forests reveal rapid but climatically lagged turnover under experimental warming**" for consideration as an Original Research article in *Global Change Biology*.

Mountain plant communities are widely reported to be reorganising under climate change, yet the evidence is dominated by resurvey and observational studies in which climate tracking cannot be separated from land-use change, nitrogen deposition and recording artefacts. Transplant experiments were designed to break exactly this confounding — treatment is assigned by the experimenter, and paired in-situ controls share site, substrate, history and observer — but their results are typically reported as a single average richness response, which conflates transient transplant shock with persistent warming effects and conceals the compositional processes beneath a near-zero net number.

Using the TransPlant network (20 mountain-grassland regions, nine countries, 41 destination sites, 225 transplanted turfs, 1,171 turf-year observations, 2007–2022) and generalised random forests, we convert this randomised but heterogeneous experiment into three quantities that species richness alone cannot deliver:

1. **A demographic mechanism.** Warming accelerates colonisation (+1.18 species per turf-year) and extinction (+0.92) by statistically indistinguishable amounts, so that compositional turnover rises by +2.08 species per turf-year (p < 0.001) while net richness does not move (joint influence-function tests; formal flux-versus-net test p = 0.021). "No net change" does not mean "no effect": composition is put on a fast conveyor belt while its species count stands still.

2. **A quantified climatic lag.** The community temperature index tracked only **2.9% (95% CI 0.0–5.9%)** of the imposed +2.4 °C warming — we believe the first experimentally identified estimate of climatic debt, in which the benchmark is set by design rather than by a resurvey baseline. Tracking strengthens from the third year onward, showing that the lag reflects the *rate at which composition can reorganise*, not the pace of climate change.

3. **An honest heterogeneity verdict.** Regional effects reverse sign (−3.98 to +3.38), but no covariate moderator survives multiple-testing correction; we report this split verdict explicitly rather than over-claiming predictable winners and losers.

We believe this work fits GCB's scope in three ways. It addresses a **global-change driver** (experimentally imposed warming) with a **mechanistic, process-level link** to community response (the colonisation–extinction accounting, with a randomised design that excludes a purely correlational interpretation, verified by a within-site placebo permutation, a positive-control replication in Nutrient Network fertilisation data, and an anti-circularity design for the thermal-preference estimates). Its conclusions are **not site-specific**: the network spans nine countries and four continents, and the finding that *averages mislead* — direction reversal among regions, decoupling of flux from net change — is itself the generalisable message for the large body of distributed warming experiments whose data already exist in public archives. Finally, the framework is immediately reusable: all data are public (OSF, EDI), all code is open (Gitee), and the analysis is reproducible end-to-end with nine R scripts.
